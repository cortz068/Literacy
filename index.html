Introduction: The Landscape of Literacy

Writing a literacy narrative that bridges your local roots, your technical projects, and your academic growth starts by picking 3 or 4 anchor moments that show how you moved from a curious kid tinkering with words and tools to a focused researcher and builder.

Instead of defining literacy as just books on a shelf, we frame it here through environment—the unique backdrop of the Rio Grande Valley, where language, environment, and early interactions with technology shaped how we made sense of the world.

1. The Early Catalyst (Place & Curiosity)

Growing up in Weslaco and the surrounding Rio Grande Valley meant being surrounded by a distinct landscape, local ecology, and a rich cultural crossroads. It was here that my early relationship with reading, observing, and questioning began.

Literacy didn't start in a sterile classroom; it began by trying to decode the environment, local conversations, and the natural mechanics of the world right outside the window.

2. From Pages to Pixels (The Digital Shift)

Getting my first device marked a massive turning point: the transition from a consumer of words and media to an active creator. Literacy expanded past the English alphabet into syntax, logic, and structure.

Writing stopped being just about essays and started being about code, scripting complex game mechanics in Luau, and building digital worlds. Debugging a broken script or writing documentation taught me precision, patience, and how to communicate instructions clearly—both to a computer and to collaborators.

3. Roots and Research (The Scientific Voice)

Transitioning into formal research, environmental science fieldwork, and data analysis changed my relationship with nonfiction and technical writing. Whether tracking local habitats or processing taproot growth data in RStudio, numbers and code became another language entirely.

Learning to translate raw data into APA-formatted research papers and ggplot2 visualizations taught me that writing is fundamentally a tool for environmental advocacy and scientific discovery.

4. Community and Collaboration (People Who Shaped the Voice)

Literacy is rarely an isolated act. Working alongside mentors like Mr. Cortez on school recycling and conservation projects, or collaborating with peers on group presentations, shifted writing from a solo school assignment to a tool for real-world communication.

Collaborating with teammates on persuasive presentations and listening under the guidance of mentors taught me how to adapt my arguments to an audience and articulate shared goals effectively.

Conclusion: The Modern Literacy Toolkit

My definition of a "literate person" has evolved dramatically. I am not just someone who reads textbooks or types essays; I am someone who builds web interfaces, analyzes ecological data in R, scripts virtual mechanics, and uses clear communication to bridge the gap between technical science and the everyday world.
